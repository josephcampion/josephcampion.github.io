Droid Arm Reach

Click (or tap, or drag) anywhere and the droid’s arm plans a smooth, straight reach to it. Hover to preview the pose. Leave it alone and it runs through a few reaches on its own.

What you’re looking at

Touch your nose. Now do it again with your elbow pointing a different way. You can, because your arm has more joints than it needs to put your finger somewhere. Roboticists call that redundancy. It’s handy, but it means the robot has to choose a pose, every instant, on its own.

This droid’s arm has three joints (shoulder, elbow and wrist), but its hand only cares about two things: left–right and up–down. Every click runs three small problems back to back:

  • Plan. Draw a straight line to the goal and decide how fast to move along it. The hand speeds up and slows down smoothly, the way yours does when you reach for a cup.
  • Pick the joints. At each instant, work out what the joints must do to keep the hand on that line. The spare joint goes toward keeping the arm in a relaxed pose.
  • Push. Work out how hard each motor has to push, accounting for gravity and for the way swinging one link yanks on the others.

The hard part is the edge of its reach. With the arm nearly straight, no joint can move the hand any farther out, and the naive math asks the motors for infinite speed. The arm has to see that coming and give up a little accuracy instead of flailing.

Things to try

  • Click near the edge of the reach and watch λ on the readout climb as the arm straightens. That’s the arm trading accuracy for sane motor speeds.
  • Drag the goal around mid-reach. The path bends smoothly instead of jerking onto a new line.
  • Watch the error charts during a long reach. The joints lag their reference and the hand drifts several millimetres off the line, then settles, but not quite to zero: the real arm is heavier than the controller thinks, so it sags a little at rest.

Under the hood: the math

A droid’s arm, seen from the side with gravity pulling down, has three joints: shoulder, elbow and wrist. Its hand only has two coordinates to hit (x and y), so there’s one joint more than it needs. Each click runs the whole chain: plan a path for the hand, turn it into joint angles, then work out the joint torques that make the arm follow it.

Forward kinematics & the Jacobian

Write φi = q1 + … + qi for the absolute angle of link i. The hand is the sum of the links laid end to end, and the Jacobian, which maps joint rates to hand velocity, comes straight from differentiating that sum:

p = Σ lk (cos φk, sin φk),   J·j = Σk≥j lk (−sin φk, cos φk),   ṗ = J q̇

Column j is what the hand does if only joint j turns: it swings everything outboard of that joint around it.

Trajectory

The hand moves along a straight line on a minimum-jerk profile, the smooth bell-shaped speed curve that human reaching follows:

pd(t) = p0 + (p1 − p0) s(τ),   s = 10τ³ − 15τ⁴ + 6τ⁵,   τ = t/T

The duration T is the shortest that keeps the peak speed (1.875 d/T) and acceleration (5.774 d/T²) under their limits. If you click again or drag mid-move, a new quintic starts from the current position, velocity and acceleration, so the path bends without a jerk. Hover to see the distance and time before you commit.

Inverse kinematics

Rather than solving for the joint angles directly, the arm integrates joint rates from the hand velocity it wants, with a feedback term to stop drift (closed-loop inverse kinematics). The rates come from a damped least-squares inverse, with a second term that uses the spare joint:

q̇d = J#(ṗd + K(pd − p)) + (I − J#J) k0(qrest − qd),   J# = Jᵀ(JJᵀ + λ²I)−1

With the arm stretched straight, every joint can only move the hand sideways, so no joint rates can move it outward: J loses rank, and a plain inverse asks for infinite speed. The damping λ trades a little accuracy for bounded rates. It only switches on near that pose, ramping in as the manipulability w = √det(JJᵀ) drops below a threshold, so it costs nothing elsewhere; watch λ on the readout when the arm reaches far. Because there are three joints for two coordinates, a whole family of poses puts the hand in the same spot. The second term picks one by gently pulling the joints toward a relaxed posture, only in directions that don’t move the hand. Joint limits keep the elbow bending one way, and a joint that hits its limit is locked while the others take up the motion.

Dynamics & control

The arm is a real rigid-body simulation: each link has mass and inertia, gravity pulls on all of them, and swinging one joint throws the others around. Computed-torque control uses a model of those dynamics to cancel them, so that, if the model were right, each joint error would behave like a simple, critically damped spring:

M(q) q̈ + h(q, q̇) = τ,   τ = M(q̈d + Kdė + Kpe) + h  ⟹  ë + Kdė + Kpe = 0

Here M is the mass matrix, h collects gravity and the Coriolis and centrifugal terms, and e = qd − q. With a perfect model, perfect sensors and ideal motors, the error would stay at zero. This arm is built so it doesn’t, the way a real one wouldn’t:

  • The model is off. The real links are 7–12% heavier than the controller thinks, the hand most of all, so gravity is under-cancelled and the arm sags a little. With no integral term, that sag never quite goes away.
  • Unmodelled friction. Each joint has viscous friction and Coulomb (dry) friction that the controller doesn’t know about. The dry friction makes the joints stick slightly as they come to rest.
  • Motors aren’t instant. Each motor’s torque follows its command with a 6 ms lag, standing in for the current loop, and saturates at its limit.
  • Sensors aren’t perfect. The controller only sees 16-bit joint encoders, and it estimates joint rates by differentiating them through a 4 ms filter.

The bars show how close each joint’s torque is to its limit. The charts show the joint errors (reference minus actual) and the hand error: up to several millimetres mid-reach, settling to a millimetre or two of sag at rest.

Numbers

Links (shoulder, elbow, wrist): l = 0.30, 0.25, 0.10 m; m = 1.5, 1.0, 0.4 kg, with the centre of mass mid-link and I = ml²/12. Torque limits 20, 8 and 1.5 N·m. Joint limits: shoulder ±135°, elbow 0–150°, wrist ±90°. Controller’s model: those masses. Real arm: 1.6, 1.08, 0.45 kg, viscous friction 0.05 N·m·s and Coulomb friction 0.10, 0.05, 0.012 N·m per joint, motor lag 6 ms. Encoders: 16 bits per turn, rate filter 4 ms. Kp = 400 /s², Kd = 40 /s. IK: K = 20 /s, λ0 = 0.05, w0 = 0.06, k0 = 2 /s, qrest = (−40°, 80°, −20°). Trajectory: vmax = 0.8 m/s, amax = 3 m/s², T ≥ 0.4 s. Goals are kept between 0.20 and 0.62 m from the shoulder and out of the droid’s body. The sim runs in real time at 500 Hz.

All values are illustrative. References: B. Siciliano, L. Sciavicco, L. Villani & G. Oriolo, Robotics: Modelling, Planning and Control, Springer, 2009; M. Spong, S. Hutchinson & M. Vidyasagar, Robot Modeling and Control; Y. Nakamura & H. Hanafusa, 1986, and C. Wampler, 1986 (damped least squares); T. Flash & N. Hogan, 1985 (minimum jerk).
