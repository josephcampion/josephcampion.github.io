X-Wing Path Following

The X-wing flies a loop of waypoints. Tap to add or remove them, and pick how it handles the corners: straight lines, fillets or Dubins paths. Clear them and it flies to your cursor instead. It’s all from Beard & McLain’s Small Unmanned Aircraft.

What you’re looking at

You can’t fly an airplane like a mouse cursor. It can’t stop, it can’t slow down much, and the only way to turn is to bank and carve an arc. So “fly to these points” really means “draw a path through them that the plane can actually fly, then stay on it.”

That splits into two layers. The bottom one is path following: given a line or a circle, stay on it, and steer back gently when you drift off. The top one is the path manager: turn the list of waypoints into lines and circles, and decide when to move on to the next one.

The three buttons are three ways to handle the corners:

  • Line. Aim straight at each waypoint. Simple, but every corner is sharper than the X-wing can turn, so it overshoots and swings back.
  • Fillet. Round each corner off with an arc it can fly. Smooth and short, but it never quite passes through the waypoints.
  • Dubins. Give each waypoint a heading too, then find the shortest turn–straight–turn path between them. For something with a minimum turn radius, that’s provably the shortest way there.

Things to try

  • Flip between Line, Fillet and Dubins on the same loop and watch what happens at the corners.
  • On Fillet, put two waypoints close together. The arc shrinks to fit, ends up tighter than the X-wing can turn, and it overshoots anyway.
  • Watch the short orange tick. It’s a finish line: the X-wing moves on as soon as it crosses, even if it missed the waypoint, instead of circling back for it.
  • Hit Clear and move your cursor around. From far away it flies a straight line at you; up close it settles into a circle around the cursor.

Where I’ve done this for real

The autopilot underneath this X-wing is a two-line toy. Real ones are most of my job. At Anduril I work on Altius, a tube-launched fixed-wing drone, and so far that’s meant the autopilot, the target state estimator, and system identification for the 6DOF sim. Before that, at Hermeus, I developed the flight controls and navigation for Quarterhorse Mk 2.1, a supersonic drone the size of an F-16.
