Death Star Orbit & Attitude

Click (or tap) anywhere to pick a new circular orbit. The Death Star turns to point its engine, burns, coasts halfway around, burns again, then turns back to aim its dish at the planet. Leave it alone and it flies one on its own.

What you’re looking at

In space there’s nothing to push against: no road, no air. To go somewhere you point your engine and burn, and to point the engine you have to turn the whole station first. So this demo is two problems stacked on top of each other: where the Death Star is going (its orbit) and which way it’s facing (its attitude).

Changing orbits. The weird part is that you don’t aim at the orbit you want. To go higher, you speed up along your path, which stretches the far side of your orbit outward. Coast halfway around to that far side, then speed up again to round it off. That’s a Hohmann transfer, the cheapest way between two circular orbits. You speed up twice and end up going slower, because higher orbits are slower. Orbits are weird.

Turning. The station turns by spinning a heavy wheel inside it the other way, the same trick as sitting on an office chair and spinning a bike wheel you’re holding. The catch: a small outside push (exaggerated here) keeps nudging the station, and the wheel has to spin a little faster every second to cancel it. Eventually it’s near top speed. So every so often, small thrusters on the rim fire to slow the wheel back down while the station holds still. That’s called momentum dumping.

Things to try

  • Hover before you click. It previews the new orbit and the ideal cost, in Δv (how much speed change the burns add up to).
  • Drop to a low orbit and compare “Δv” with “ideal” on the readout. Real burns take time instead of happening instantly, so they always cost a bit more.
  • Watch the wheel momentum bar fill up, then drain when the rim thrusters kick in (the mode shows DESAT). The pointing barely moves while it happens.

Under the hood: the math

Two things are simulated together, in 2D: where the Death Star is (its orbit, x and y) and which way it’s facing (its attitude, θ). Thrust only pushes along the dish axis, so the attitude has to be right before the orbit can change.

Orbit

Plain two-body gravity, plus engine thrust along the body axis when it’s firing:

r̈ = −μ r / |r|³ + athrust (cos θ, sin θ)

The solid ellipse is the current orbit, worked out from position and velocity every frame. Hover to preview a new orbit and the ideal Δv to reach it.

Hohmann transfer

The cheapest way between two circular orbits is two burns, half an orbit apart. To go up: burn prograde to stretch the orbit until its far point (apoapsis) reaches the new radius, coast to that point, then burn prograde again to round it out. Going down is the same, burning retrograde. The ideal, instantaneous-burn cost is

Δv = |vp − v1| + |v2 − va|,   vcirc = √(μ/r),   v = √(μ(2/r − 1/a))

Each burn here is a real, finite burn, and it ends on the live orbit rather than a timer. Burn 1 stops when the far side of the orbit reaches the target radius. Burn 2 steers along the exact velocity change that would make the orbit circular, and stops when there’s nothing left to fix. Because the burns take time instead of happening at a point, the Δv used comes out a little above the ideal, most noticeably when dropping to a low, fast orbit.

Pointing

The Death Star turns by spinning a reaction wheel the other way. The angle error sets a turn-rate command (capped at 3°/s), the rate error sets the wheel torque, and a small integral term trims out steady disturbances:

J θ̈ = u + τext,   ωc = sat(kθ eθ) + ωff,   u = J (kω(ωc − ω) + kI∫eθ)

The target direction is always moving, whether that’s the planet below or the direction of travel, so the guidance feeds forward how fast its command is turning (ωff). Without that, the station lags a few degrees behind and never lines up well enough to burn. The engine only fires within 2° of the burn direction.

Momentum dumping

A constant disturbance torque (exaggerated so you don’t wait all day) keeps pushing on the station. To hold still, the wheel absorbs it and slowly spins up. Watch the wheel momentum bar fill. At 75%, small thrusters on the rim fire against the stored momentum while the attitude loop holds steady, draining the wheel down to 10%. The controller feeds that thruster torque forward too, so the pointing barely moves.

Numbers

μ = 4.6 × 10⁶ km³/s² (picked so a 3000 km orbit takes 8 minutes), planet radius 1000 km, engine 0.8 km/s². Attitude: J = 10 kg·m², Jw = 0.002 kg·m², umax = 0.1 N·m, Ωmax = 6000 rpm, ωmax = 3°/s, kθ = 0.15 /s, kω = 0.6 /s, kI = 0.01 /s², τdist = 8 mN·m, desaturation thrusters 30 mN·m. The sim runs at 100 Hz and twelve times faster than real time. The station isn’t drawn to scale.

All values are illustrative. The attitude numbers are sized for a small satellite; a moon-sized battle station would need a much bigger wheel. References: B. Wie, Space Vehicle Dynamics and Control, 2nd ed., AIAA, 2008; H. Curtis, Orbital Mechanics for Engineering Students.
