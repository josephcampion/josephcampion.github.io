Millennium Falcon: Turbojet to Ramjet

Click (or tap) anywhere to set a target Mach number; left is slow, right is fast, lined up with the chart along the bottom. The freighter lights its afterburner, climbs as it speeds up, and somewhere past Mach 2 swings a door in its inlet to hand the air from the turbojet to the ramjet. It holds whatever Mach you last picked until you click again.

What you’re looking at

A jet engine is basically a fancy hair dryer: squeeze air, heat it, and blast it out the back faster than it came in. The question is what does the squeezing.

  • A turbojet squeezes with a spinning compressor. It works from a standstill, but as you go faster the air arrives already hot from slamming into the inlet. Past about Mach 3 the compressor can’t add much without cooking itself, and the engine runs out of breath.
  • A ramjet has no moving parts. Going supersonic, the inlet squeezes the air just by slowing it down. It shrugs off speeds that kill a turbojet, but standing still it does exactly nothing.

So this ship carries both, stacked one over the other and sharing one inlet. Between Mach 2 and 3, a door in the inlet swings over and hands the air from the turbojet to the ramjet. The tricky spot is right before that hand-off: the turbojet is fading and the ramjet hasn’t really woken up yet, so thrust barely beats drag. Engineers call it the pinch point. A ship that can’t push through it never gets to ramjet speed.

Things to try

  • Click near the right edge to ask for Mach 4 and watch the engine diagram: afterburner, then the door swinging over, then ramjet only.
  • Watch the thrust chart around Mach 2. That narrow gap between thrust and drag is the pinch point.
  • Keep an eye on the compressor numbers under the engine diagram (πc is how hard it squeezes, Tt3 is how hot the air leaving it gets). As speed climbs, the squeeze drops and the heat rises.
  • Flip the chart between “Along the climb” and “At current altitude” to see how much of the story is the thinning air.

Where I’ve done this for real

At Hermeus I worked on Quarterhorse, an F-16-sized drone built to reach Mach 2.5 and beyond. Along with flight controls and navigation, I designed propulsion controls that reached Mach 3 conditions in testing at the Notre Dame Turbomachinery Lab. Real Engineering made a video about Hermeus if you want the bigger picture.

The engine models here come from MIT’s 16.50, Introduction to Propulsion Systems, which I worked through on my own.
