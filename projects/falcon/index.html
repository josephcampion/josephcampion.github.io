Millennium Falcon: Turbojet to Ramjet

Click (or tap) anywhere to set a target Mach number; left is slow, right is fast, lined up with the chart along the bottom. The freighter lights its afterburner, climbs as it speeds up, and somewhere past Mach 2 swings a door in its inlet to hand the air from the turbojet to the ramjet. Leave it alone and it flies up to Mach 3.8 and back on its own.

What’s going on

A jet engine squeezes air, adds heat, and throws the hot gas out the back faster than it came in. A turbojet does the squeezing with a compressor, driven by a turbine. A ramjet has no moving parts: above about Mach 2 the inlet slows the incoming air enough to squeeze it on its own. The catch is that a ramjet does nothing standing still, and a turbojet runs out of breath going fast. So this ship carries both, one above the other, sharing one inlet. That arrangement is called an over/under combined cycle.

Turbojet

Each engine is the ideal cycle from a propulsion textbook: lossless components, a perfectly expanded nozzle, and constant gas properties. For the turbojet, with τr = 1 + ½(γ−1)M0² the ram temperature ratio and τλ = Tt4/T0 the burner temperature over ambient,

(V9/a0)² = 2/(γ−1) · τλ/(τrτc) · (τrτcτt − 1),   F = ṁ a0 (V9/a0 − M0)

With the afterburner lit, τλ/(τrτc) becomes Tt7/(T0τrτcτt). The afterburner adds about a third more thrust, at a steep cost in fuel.

The interesting part is off-design. The engine is sized standing still at sea level, and with a choked turbine its compressor work is tied to the ratio of burner temperature to inlet temperature:

τc − 1 ∝ Tt4/Tt2,   ṁ ∝ (Pt2/√Tt2) · πc / √(Tt4/Tt2)

Going faster heats the air before it even reaches the compressor (Tt2 climbs with M²). The compressor unloads, and its pressure ratio πc sags from 12 at takeoff to about 4 at Mach 3. Meanwhile the compressor exit temperature Tt3 keeps rising. Holding Tt3 under its 900 K limit means turning the burner down, and by Mach 3.6 there’s nothing left. Watch πc and Tt3 under the engine diagram.

Ramjet

Take the compressor and turbine out (τc = τt = 1), and the same equation gets much simpler:

F = ṁ a0 M0 (√(τλ/τr) − 1),   ṁ = ρ0 V0 Ac

At zero speed, ṁ is zero and so is the thrust. With no turbine downstream, the burner can also run hotter: 2400 K here, against 1700 K for the turbojet.

The hand-off

From Mach 2.0 to 3.0, a door at the front of the splitter swings from sealing the ramjet duct to sealing the turbojet duct, easing the inlet’s air from one engine to the other. Both run in between. The chart shows why the band sits where it does. Below Mach 2 the turbojet is far more fuel-efficient: its specific impulse is above 2500 s, while the ideal ramjet manages under 1900 s. Above Mach 3 the turbojet is temperature-limited and fading, and the ramjet is the only thing still climbing. The narrowest gap between thrust and drag, the pinch point, comes just before the door starts to move. A ship that can’t get through it never reaches ramjet speed.

Flying it

The ship is a point mass. Lift balances weight, and the flight-path angle γ follows its command with a short lag:

V̇ = (F − D)/m − g sin γ,   ḣ = V sin γ

It climbs on a constant dynamic pressure schedule, the usual way to fly an air-breather. Since q = ½ρV² = ½γair p M², holding q at 45 kPa means the higher the Mach, the higher the altitude (the dashed mark on the altitude tape). A PI loop on Mach error sets the single power lever: below 60% it sets turbojet burner temperature, and above that it lights the afterburner. The ramjet’s burner follows the whole lever.

Numbers

Mass 30 t, wing area 60 m², zero-lift drag coefficient 0.016 subsonic, peaking at 0.042 just past Mach 1. Turbojet: πc = 12 and 150 kg/s at sea-level static, Tt4 up to 1700 K, afterburner 2200 K, Tt3 ≤ 900 K. Ramjet: capture area 1.6 m², Tt4 up to 2400 K. Fuel heating value 42.8 MJ/kg. Standard atmosphere, Mach 2–3 door schedule, q = 45 kPa with a 2 km floor. The sim runs at 50 Hz and twenty times faster than real time.

All values are illustrative, and every engine model is the textbook ideal cycle. Real inlets lose pressure through shocks, which hurts the ramjet most at low Mach, and real hand-offs have to manage inlet stability and relighting. The ship is an original drawing. References: J. Mattingly, Elements of Propulsion: Gas Turbines and Rockets, 2nd ed., AIAA, 2006; P. Hill & C. Peterson, Mechanics and Thermodynamics of Propulsion, 2nd ed.
